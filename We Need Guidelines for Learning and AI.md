> [!QUOTE] Original Capture
> AI creates both an enormous opportunity and a serious risk for learning. It can provide cheap tutoring, explanations, practice problems, research assistance, and feedback—but if it simply does the work for you, **you lose the practice required to actually develop the skill**. The danger isn't automation itself, but becoming dependent on it before you've built the underlying competence. ([Scott H. Young][1])
>
> ### **What is the Right “Amount” of AI Use for Optimal Learning?**
>
> Too much AI clearly hurts learning: if your goal is to learn programming, having an agent write the code means you're not practicing programming yourself. But **too little assistance can also hurt learning**.
>
> Struggling with difficult problems is important, but cognitive load can become so high that you're merely floundering. At that point, a worked example, partial solution, explanation, or hint can teach you faster than continued unaided struggle.
>
> The right amount of AI assistance also depends on expertise. **Beginners need direct practice to build mental models; experts can benefit more from delegation because they already possess the knowledge needed to understand and steer the output.** ([Scott H. Young][1])
>
> ### **Some Tentative Guidelines**
>
> Young stresses that these are tentative principles rather than established scientific consensus, since AI is too new for education research to have settled on best practices. ([Scott H. Young][1])
>
> #### **1. You can’t learn from work you don’t do yourself.**
>
> AI assistance may improve learning, but completely delegating a task means you aren't practicing the underlying skill. **If you want to acquire a capability, some of the work must remain yours.**
>
> #### **2. Don’t use AI on your first attempt.**
>
> Try solving the problem yourself before asking AI. The initial struggle forces you to think, form hypotheses, and develop judgment.
>
> This is particularly important for creative work. If AI starts an essay for you, for example, its choices immediately constrain your thinking. You miss the experience of deciding what direction to pursue and how to construct the argument yourself.
>
> #### **3. Don’t use AI on your last attempt.**
>
> If you're stuck after genuinely trying, **use AI for help rather than struggling indefinitely**. Worked examples and explanations can be more educational than continued failure.
>
> But don't stop there. After AI helps you understand the problem, attempt another similar problem **without AI**. That final independent attempt establishes whether you actually learned the skill.
>
> #### **4. Do use AI to suggest alternative ideas and resources.**
>
> Once you've thought about a problem yourself, AI's breadth becomes valuable. Ask it to surface approaches, methods, papers, books, or ideas you overlooked. This lets AI **expand your thinking rather than substitute for it**.
>
> #### **5. Do use AI to fix confusion and mistakes.**
>
> After completing your own attempt, AI can act as a source of feedback: compare your solution with a stronger one, identify errors, explain why you're confused, and show where your reasoning went wrong. ([Scott H. Young][1])
>
> ### **See-Do-Feedback (+AI)**
>
> Young frames effective learning as a repeating loop:
>
> **See → Do → Feedback**
>
> **See:** Learn from instruction, examples, explanations, or demonstrations.
> **Do:** Practice the skill yourself.
> **Feedback:** Discover what worked, what didn't, and how to improve.
> 
> ![[Pasted image 20260730021215.png]]
>
> AI can dramatically improve **See** by providing personalized explanations and examples, and **Feedback** by analyzing mistakes and answering questions.
>
> But it cannot replace **Do**. The useful role for AI is therefore *around* independent practice rather than *instead of* it: **AI → practice yourself → AI feedback → practice yourself again.** ([Scott H. Young][1])
