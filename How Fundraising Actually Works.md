> ### **The Theory of Adverse Selection**
>
> Early-stage fundraising is fundamentally an information problem. Founders know far more about their startups than investors do, creating **adverse selection**: investors become suspicious that startups actively seeking capital may have already been rejected by others. This is why open fundraising marketplaces and public pitching events tend to attract weaker opportunities—they unintentionally signal that the best companies have already found funding elsewhere.
>
> ### **Why Cold Outreach Doesn't Work**
>
> Cold emails rarely succeed because investors receive enormous volumes of unsolicited pitches. A cold approach provides no external validation, reinforcing the adverse selection problem. As a result, most cold outreach is ignored regardless of the startup's quality.
>
> ### **Why Warm Introductions Also Don't Work**
>
> Warm introductions improve the chance that an investor reads the email, but they don't solve the core issue. If a founder is actively asking for investment, investors may still wonder why no one else has already committed. This often leads to slow responses, delayed meetings, and prolonged fundraising processes as investors wait for others to validate the opportunity first.
>
> ### **How Fundraising Actually Works — Backchanneling**
>
> The article argues that the most effective strategy is **backchanneling**. Instead of requesting an introduction, founders should encourage trusted mutual contacts to casually mention them to investors as impressive founders who may raise soon. This creates investor curiosity without making the founder appear to be actively seeking capital. If investors initiate the conversation themselves, the founder avoids triggering adverse selection and fundraising can move much faster, sometimes without even needing a pitch deck.
>
> ### **Wrapping Up**
>
> The key recommendations are:
>
> * Avoid public signals that you're actively fundraising.
> * Don't rely on cold outreach or traditional warm introductions.
> * Use trusted relationships to generate **inbound investor interest** through backchanneling.
>
> The article concludes that fundraising is largely driven by **perception and signaling**. The strongest long-term advantage is building a reputation through consistently excellent work and strong relationships, making investors seek you out rather than the other way around.
