> ### **AI Might Bring Craftsmanship Back**
>
> Modern capitalism often rewards **high margins, low costs, rapid product cycles, and scale** more than durability or craftsmanship. Companies can eventually be pushed toward cheaper mass production because consumers rarely pay proportionally more for incremental improvements in quality.
>
> AI could disrupt this dynamic. By discovering better materials, processes, designs, and manufacturing improvements, it may make **higher quality dramatically cheaper to produce**. AI could expose products whose price or reputation isn't justified by their underlying quality and potentially trigger a resurgence in craftsmanship. ([Michael Novati][1])
>
> ### **AI Is Calling Every Bluff**
>
> The larger argument extends beyond physical products: **anything normal, repeatable, explainable, or reducible to a process is becoming less valuable.**
>
> Jobs often contain tasks whose perceived difficulty, expertise, or economic value exceeds what is actually required to perform them. AI exposes that gap. This applies both to mundane corporate work and highly paid expertise: once an AI can learn a repeatable skill, humans who built careers around that skill must find another way to create value.
>
> This isn't necessarily because people were intentionally gaming the system. People adapted to existing incentives and built careers and identities around what society rewarded. But AI doesn't care about those arrangements—it simply reveals what can be reproduced cheaply. ([Michael Novati][1])
>
> ### **AI Could Remove Performative Work**
>
> Many economic interactions exist partly because humans historically had no better mechanism. A salesperson spends 30 minutes persuading you because they need to communicate information, build trust, and influence your decision.
>
> If your AI understands your needs and another AI understands a product perfectly, they could simply determine whether there's a match. Much of the **performance, persuasion, manufactured urgency, organizational politics, and visibility theater** surrounding work could become unnecessary.
>
> The uncomfortable question is whether society is prepared for that. Existing systems often reward conformity and performance rather than simply rewarding people for producing the best possible outcome. ([Michael Novati][1])
>
> ### **The Grim Reaper Applies to Everyone**
>
> Novati turns the argument on himself: everyone has work they inflate, excuses they make, and abilities they hope are more unique than they really are. AI may reveal that many things we considered personal expertise are reproducible.
>
> Unlike humans, AI doesn't have insecurity, exhaustion, embarrassment, or self-protection. But it also lacks the strange obsessions, passions, personal histories, and intrinsic desires that shape human beings.
>
> That leads to Novati's tentative bet: **the things most worth investing in may be the things uniquely yours**—what fascinates you, what you want to understand more deeply than anyone else, and what you would continue doing even if nobody paid you for it. ([Michael Novati][1])
>
> ### **AI Will Challenge More Than Work**
>
> The same process could eventually challenge deeply embedded social institutions and assumptions around relationships, family, religion, morality, and identity. If AI and technology provide alternatives to institutions that historically fulfilled important human needs, people may begin questioning whether those structures are necessary at all.
>
> Novati sees this possibility as simultaneously **liberating and terrifying**: people might gain more freedom to decide who they want to be without having to conform to existing definitions of usefulness, productivity, or morality. ([Michael Novati][1])
>
> ### **The Question**
>
> The essay deliberately avoids predicting whether this future will be good or bad. Instead, it asks what remains when AI calls the bluffs embedded in our products, careers, institutions, and identities.
>
> **What do you actually want to do? What do you enjoy when nobody is watching? What would you still care about if it had no economic value? And if AI really becomes as capable as we imagine, what role do you want to have in that world?** ([Michael Novati][1])
