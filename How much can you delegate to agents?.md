> ### **The Two Factors That Set the Ceiling**
>
> How much autonomy you should give an agent depends less on **how intelligent the model is** and more on the characteristics of the task. Two questions determine the appropriate level:
>
> 1. **Is the agent’s work easy to check?** Deterministic feedback—tests, measurable outcomes, automated validation—makes autonomy safer. Subjective work requiring taste or judgment is harder to delegate fully.
> 2. **Is a mistake cheap to undo?** Autonomous work needs a reliable “Ctrl+Z.” The greater the blast radius or irreversibility of a mistake, the more supervision is warranted.
>
> Combining these dimensions produces four levels of autonomy. ([PostHog Newsletter][1])
> 
> ![[Pasted image 20260729210123.png]]
>
> ### **Level 0: Agent as Assistant**
>
> **Hard to check + costly to undo.**
>
> For sensitive, complex work where mistakes have a large blast radius, the human should remain closely involved. Agents can still handle isolated, lower-risk pieces while the human owns the critical parts.
>
> **How to level up:** Decompose large tasks into smaller units. This reveals which pieces are actually safe to delegate rather than treating the entire project as equally risky. ([PostHog Newsletter][1])
>
> ### **Level 1: Human-in-the-Loop**
>
> **Hard to check + cheap to undo.**
>
> Tasks requiring taste or subjective judgment can be generated by agents but should remain drafts until humans evaluate them. Since nothing is committed yet, failure simply means another iteration.
>
> **How to level up:** Automate judgment where possible using **LLM-as-judge**, turn subjective goals into measurable criteria, and create custom skills that encode your team's standards and preferences. ([PostHog Newsletter][1])
> 
> ![[Pasted image 20260729210236.png]]
>
> ### **Level 2: Agent Delegation**
>
> **Easy to check + costly to undo.**
>
> This is where most software-development tasks currently sit. Agents can produce work autonomously because tests provide reliable verification, but consequential actions—such as deploying or merging high-impact changes—still require safety gates.
>
> **How to level up:** Stop making humans the default safety mechanism. Encode guardrails directly into the system through policies such as dry runs, scoped credentials, feature flags, staged rollouts, and other automated safeguards. ([PostHog Newsletter][1])
> 
> ![[Pasted image 20260729210301.png]]
>
> ### **Level 3: Self-Driving Mode**
>
> **Easy to check + cheap to undo.**
>
> Agents can operate end-to-end when both verification and recovery are reliable. Today this works best for relatively constrained tasks such as dependency updates, lint fixes, and adding test coverage, but the category is expanding as long-running agents and orchestration improve.
>
> **How to level up:** Improve autonomous systems with **domain-specific models**, rich and up-to-date **context banks**, and clear signals that tell long-running agents when meaningful work exists rather than having them react to noise. ([PostHog Newsletter][1])
>
> ### **The Core Idea**
>
> Don't decide agent autonomy based on how impressive the latest model seems. **Engineer autonomy around the task.** Make work easier to verify and mistakes easier to reverse, and more tasks naturally move toward self-driving execution. Scale isn't what determines autonomy; getting these properties right at the individual task level is what enables safe scaling. ([PostHog Newsletter][1])
