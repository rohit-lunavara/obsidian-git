> [!QUOTE] Original Capture
> ### **Part 1 — Why Not to Do a Startup**
>
> Startups offer autonomy, creativity, impact, culture-building, and potentially enormous financial rewards—but they come with extreme emotional volatility, rejection, brutal hiring, long hours, cultural problems, and unpredictable external risks. Most importantly, **nothing happens unless you make it happen**; startups lack the machinery established companies have to keep things moving automatically. ([PM Archive][1])
>
> ### **Part 2 — When the VCs Say “No”**
>
> A few rejections mean little, but repeated rejection means you should stop pitching and **remove layers of risk**—team, market, competition, technology, product, financing, distribution, etc.—before trying again. The highest-value ways to de-risk are usually **building the product and getting customers**, then returning to investors with new facts rather than a better argument. ([PM Archive][2])
>
> ### **Part 3 — “But I Don’t Know Any VCs!”**
>
> VCs rely heavily on referrals because they cannot evaluate every startup that approaches them, so founders should build relationships with people in the venture ecosystem rather than rely on unsolicited pitches. Before networking, **have your stuff together**: ideally bring a working product and traction, otherwise a strong prototype and well-researched pitch—and target VCs who actually invest in your category. ([PM Archive][3])
>
> ### **Part 4 — The Only Thing That Matters**
>
> Of **team, product, and market**, market matters most: a great market pulls a viable product from a startup, while even an exceptional team and product can fail in a market without sufficient demand. The overriding goal is **product/market fit**; before PMF, do whatever is necessary to reach it, because nearly everything else is secondary. ([PM Archive][4])
>
> ### **Part 5 — The Moby Dick Theory of Big Companies**
>
> When dealing with a large company, think of yourself as Captain Ahab and the corporation as **Moby Dick**: its behavior is controlled by a huge, opaque network of people, politics, incentives, and internal processes that you cannot reliably predict or control. Never make your startup dependent on a big company doing what it says it will do, on the timeline you expect. ([PM Archive][5])
>
> ### **Part 6 — How Much Funding Is Too Little? Too Much?**
>
> Before PMF, raise enough to reach PMF; after PMF, raise enough to fully exploit the opportunity and reach profitability—with substantial extra runway for unexpected problems and closed funding markets. Andreessen's general rule is to **raise as much as you reasonably can when you can**, without surrendering control or behaving irresponsibly, because running out of money is existential. ([PM Archive][6])
>
> ### **Part 7 — Why a Startup’s Initial Business Plan Doesn’t Matter That Much**
>
> Your original plan will probably be wrong because startups operate under extreme uncertainty; what matters is **rapidly adapting toward a large market and product/market fit** as reality provides new information. Successful companies often emerge from pivots and unexpected discoveries, so flexibility and responsiveness beat detailed upfront planning. ([PM Archive][7])
>
> ### **Part 8 — Hiring, Managing, Promoting, and Firing Executives**
>
> Startup executives must simultaneously **run their organization, build it, contribute individually, and work well with the rest of the company**. Hire executives only when genuinely needed, hire for the next ~9 months rather than some imagined future company, promote strong internal people when possible, and favor hungry builders over prestigious big-company résumés. ([PM Archive][8])
>
> ### **Part 9 — How to Hire a Professional CEO**
>
> **Don't.** If nobody on the founding team is capable of being CEO, Andreessen's advice is to sell the company rather than bring in a professional CEO to solve the problem. ([PM Archive][9])
